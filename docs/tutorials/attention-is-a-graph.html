Sequel to “Flattening 768 Dimensions”

Attention is a graph

Not like a graph. Is one. And once you see that, a transformer's forward pass and a knowledge map turn out to be solving the same problem: a fixed set of things, a changing set of weights, and a budget of attention too small to spend everywhere.

Prerequisite: the first tutorial, or comfort with dot products and softmax.
Everything here computes live on embeddings derived from tessera's own corpus — 69,827 tokens across 318 chunks.
Contains a game you will probably lose to a trigram model, which is the point.

The previous tutorial ended on a question: a layout is a choice, and it could be made relative to the state of the person looking. What would guide that choice?

This one comes at it sideways, through a system that already solves a version of it several hundred times per token.

01

The claim, stated precisely

A transformer computes, for every pair of tokens, a number saying how much one should draw on the other. Stack those numbers into a matrix and you have an n × n array of weights over n items.

That is an adjacency matrix. Tokens are nodes; attention weights are edges. A transformer's attention pattern is a weighted directed graph, and a forward pass is a sequence of such graphs over one fixed node set. A twelve-layer, twelve-head model produces 144 of them for a single input.

This is not an analogy that needs constructing. It is a description of the object.

One word, two meanings — do not let them merge

Perceptual attention is what a person looks at. Transformer attention is softmax(QKᵀ/√d), a normalised similarity between learned projections. They share a name because both allocate a limited budget across many candidates, and that shared structure is real and interesting. They are not the same phenomenon, and any argument that slides between them without saying so is cheating. This tutorial uses the second sense unless it says otherwise.

02

Watch one get built

Here is the whole computation, in four steps, for a sentence drawn from tessera's own design docs.

  1. Each token starts as a vector. These are real — 16-dimensional vectors built from positive pointwise mutual information over a ±4-word window across the corpus, then reduced by the same power-iteration method the first tutorial used for PCA. In this corpus store ends up nearest to owns, identity and mapper; chunk nearest to dedups, content and hash. The attention below is therefore attending to something, not to noise.
  2. Three projections turn each vector into a query ("what am I looking for?"), a key ("what do I offer?"), and a value ("what do I pass on?").
  3. Every query meets every key: score(i,j) = qᵢ · kⱼ / √d. Divide by √d or the dot products grow with dimension and the softmax saturates.
  4. Softmax each row so it sums to 1. That row is now a probability distribution over what token i attends to — and the whole matrix is your graph.

Turn the causal mask off and watch every token gain the ability to look forward. That single triangular constraint is most of the difference between a model that generates text and a model that classifies it.

Now the part that matters for a map. Each layer produces a different graph over the same nodes. Which is exactly the dynamic-graph problem: how do you show a viewer that the edges changed without moving everything and destroying what they had learned? Attention visualisation converged on the same answer the map did — hold the geometry fixed, vary the emphasis. Tokens keep their positions; only the arcs change. Two fields arriving at one answer from different directions is mild evidence it is the right one.

03

Attention is a budget, and so is a map

Softmax is not a scoring function. It is a rationing function. Its rows sum to one, so attending more to one token necessarily means attending less to another. There is no configuration in which a transformer attends fully to everything.

A map has the same constraint, imposed by a different scarcity. A screen has finite pixels and a viewer has finite foveal attention, so emphasis given to one node is taken from another. When tessera's viewer gained a control for how hard weakly-connected nodes fade at altitude, the name that fit was attention budget — the same phrase already used for its edge filter. That was not borrowed from transformers; it arrived because the constraint is genuinely the same shape.

Where the analogy stops, and it stops hard

A transformer learns its allocation from data, against a loss. A map's allocation is designed, by a person, against a guess. That asymmetry is the whole difficulty of the design question, and no amount of structural similarity dissolves it. A transformer knows what its attention is for because gradient descent told it. A map designer has to decide.

04

Surprisal: the currency both systems trade in

Here is the thread that joins attention, novelty, and the question of what should guide a layout.

When a model assigns probability p to the token that actually appears, the surprisal of that event is −log₂ p bits. Confident and correct costs nearly nothing. Confident and wrong is expensive. Surprisal is the currency of prediction, and it turns out to be the currency of attention too:

  • In psycholinguistics, surprisal predicts reading time — one of the more robust findings in the field. Unexpected words cost the reader measurable milliseconds.
  • In vision, Itti & Baldi's Bayesian surprise work argues that gaze is drawn by the KL divergence between what you believed before an observation and after it. Not by brightness or motion as such — by belief change.
  • Which gives a design principle for a map that adapts to a viewer, and it is not "show what is relevant". Move the geometry in proportion to prediction error. Something that slots into what is already there changes little, correctly, because you already knew it. Something that does not fit forces movement, and the movement is the notification.

This inverts the failure mode of relevance-driven adaptation. A layout that tracks your interests is a filter bubble made of geometry: it brings you closer to what you already lean toward and quietly loses the ability to surprise you. A layout that tracks prediction error spends its motion exactly on what you did not expect. And it earns stability rather than trading it away — an unsurprising update moves nothing precisely because it was unsurprising, which makes stillness meaningful instead of merely preserved.

The trap, named before it bites

Novelty and noise are indistinguishable from inside the metric. A garbled passage, a parse failure, a badly-extracted generic entity — each is maximally surprising and entirely junk. A surprise-driven map built naively would devote its most dramatic motion to the worst data it holds. Any real version needs a quality term, and the first honest experiment is to check whether measured surprise correlates with extraction quality before believing any of it.

05

Play Shannon's game

Which raises a measurement question: surprisal relative to whose model? A model's surprisal is computable. A person's is not, directly — you cannot ask someone to emit a probability distribution over 6,795 tokens.

Shannon solved this in 1951. In Prediction and Entropy of Printed English he had people guess successive letters of a text and used the number of guesses required to bound the entropy of English. That is the trick: you cannot get a distribution out of a person, but you can get a ranking, and ranking is enough. If someone reliably gets it on the first guess, their implied distribution was sharp. If it takes eleven, it was flat.

The interesting quantity is not who wins. It is where the gap falls. Shannon measured the entropy of English in general. The live question for a personal knowledge graph is the entropy of your own corpus, to you — and the places where you and a model disagree about what is predictable are a map of what you know that it does not.

That difference is also, conveniently, the prior a surprise map needs. If you want geometry to move in proportion to prediction error, something must hold the prediction.

06

What this game does not measure

Being clear about it, because the appealing version of this idea is wrong in a specific way.

A tempting framing is "get a human to be a transformer". That does not work, and the reason is worth understanding. A transformer does not pick a token. It emits a full distribution over the vocabulary; choosing one is a separate step performed by a decoder. A person producing a single word gives you one sample, and you cannot recover a distribution from single samples without many trials over the same context — which no one will sit through.

The guesses-to-correct trick is the workaround, and it is why Shannon's design has survived seventy-five years. But note what it buys and what it does not:

MeasuredNot measured
A bound on your predictive entropy for this textWhich words you attended to while predicting
Where you and the model disagreeWhether your mechanism resembles attention at all
How predictable this corpus is, to you, todayAnything about a real transformer's internals

The last column matters. Nothing here licenses the claim that human prediction works like attention. The honest position is that both systems allocate a scarce resource under uncertainty, and surprisal is a currency in which both can be denominated. That is a real shared structure. It is not an identity, and treating it as one would be the same error as sliding between the two senses of "attention" in section 01.

Where a genuinely new measurement might live

Not in comparing a human to a transformer. In comparing a human to a model over a corpus that human authored. Shannon measured English, which nobody owns. A personal knowledge graph is the rare case where the reader has privileged priors over the exact text being predicted — and the gap between those priors and a model's is a measurement of expertise that has, as far as I can find, no established instrument.
