Dimensionality reduction, worked on real data

01

A projection is a choice about what to preserve

Hold your hand between a lamp and the wall. The shadow is a projection: three dimensions squeezed into two. You lose depth entirely, and you lose it in a way that depends on how you hold your hand. Rotate it and the same hand casts a completely different silhouette. Neither shadow is wrong. Neither is complete.

Now make that precise. A linear projection picks a direction — a unit vector w — and replaces each data point x with a single number, the dot product x · w. That number says how far along w the point sits. Pick two perpendicular directions and each point gets two numbers: a screen position. That is the whole mechanism.

So the design question becomes concrete: which two directions? There are infinitely many, and they are not equally good. Below are your 260 nodes under three different answers.

The arbitrary projection is not a strawman — it is what you get if you just grab dimensions 0 and 1. Notice it is not noise; it is a blob. Nearly every individual dimension of an embedding is close to meaningless on its own, because the information lives in combinations of dimensions rather than in any one of them.

02

PCA: keep the directions where things differ most

Principal Component Analysis answers "which two directions?" with a specific criterion: keep the directions of greatest variance. The intuition is that a direction along which all your points look the same tells you nothing about them, and a direction along which they spread out tells you a lot.

The recipe, in four steps you could implement this afternoon:

  1. Centre the data. Subtract the mean vector from every point. PCA is about variation about the mean, and skipping this makes the first component point at the mean itself.
  2. Find the direction of maximum variance. Formally the top eigenvector of the covariance matrix. Practically you can get it by power iteration: start with any vector, repeatedly multiply by the covariance and renormalise, and it converges to the dominant direction. That is literally how the numbers on this page were computed.
  3. Remove it and repeat. Subtract each point's component along that direction ("deflation"), then find the top direction of what remains. It is automatically perpendicular to the first.
  4. Project. Two dot products per point.

PCA has real virtues that get forgotten in the rush to fancier methods. It is deterministic — same input, same output, forever. It is linear, so distances along a component mean something consistent everywhere in the picture. And critically for you, it is a function: once you have the directions, a brand-new node can be projected without recomputing anything or moving any existing point.

Why that last property matters here

Your design has a hard rule that a map someone has learned must stay put. PCA satisfies it natively. Most of the methods in the rest of this tutorial do not, and that turns out to matter more than accuracy.

And yet: 8.3%. Why so poor?

03

Why linear is not enough: crowding

Imagine points scattered on the surface of a sphere in 3-d. A linear projection is a shadow, so the front and back of the sphere land on top of each other. Points that were maximally far apart — antipodes — come out adjacent. No choice of viewing angle fixes it, because the problem is not the angle; it is that a flat shadow cannot represent a curved surface.

Real embedding spaces are far worse than a sphere, and there is a counting argument for why. In two dimensions a point can have maybe six neighbours all equidistant from it and from each other. In 768 dimensions it can have hundreds. When you flatten, those hundreds have to fit into a neighbourhood with room for six. Everything gets pressed together and the fine structure — which is exactly the structure you wanted — is crushed. This is called the crowding problem, and it is the reason non-linear methods exist.

The move that dissolves it is to stop trying to preserve distances at all. Preserve neighbourhoods instead: don't ask "how far is A from B", ask "is B among A's closest companions?" Give up global geometry, buy back local structure.

04

t-SNE: turn neighbourhoods into probabilities

t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (van der Maaten & Hinton, 2008) is the method that made this idea famous. It works in three moves.

Move one: neighbourhood as a probability

For each point i in the original high-dimensional space, define the probability that it would "pick" point j as its neighbour. Nearby points get high probability, distant ones get almost none, using a Gaussian centred on i. Every point gets its own Gaussian width, tuned so that each ends up with roughly the same effective number of neighbours. That target count is the perplexity — the one parameter you actually have to think about. It is, loosely, "how many neighbours count as a neighbourhood."

This per-point width is a quietly brilliant detail. A point in a dense region gets a narrow Gaussian and a point out in the sparse hinterland gets a wide one, so both end up describing a comparable-sized social circle. Density differences stop dominating the result.

Move two: the same thing in 2-d, but with heavier tails

Define the same kind of probability for the two-dimensional picture — but using a Student t-distribution with one degree of freedom (a Cauchy) instead of a Gaussian. That is the "t" in t-SNE and it is precisely the fix for crowding. A t-distribution has fat tails, so a moderately large distance in 2-d still yields a respectable probability. Points that must be pushed far apart to make room are not punished as harshly as a Gaussian would punish them.

Move three: make the two match

Now move the 2-d points around until their neighbourhood probabilities look like the high-dimensional ones. "Look like" is the Kullback–Leibler divergence, and you minimise it by gradient descent. Each point feels an attraction to its true neighbours and a repulsion from everything else — which, satisfyingly, is the same force-directed physics your layout engine already runs, only with springs derived from the embedding rather than from edges.

Press Run and watch it happen on your own nodes.

Try perplexity 5, then 50. The picture changes character completely — low perplexity finds many small tight clumps, high perplexity finds fewer, broader groupings. Neither is the truth. They are answers to different questions about what counts as "nearby."

05

What t-SNE does not preserve, and why people misread it constantly

This is the section that matters most, because t-SNE plots are misinterpreted in published papers on a regular basis. Three things in a t-SNE picture are not information:

What you seeWhat you may concludeActual status
Two clusters are far apartThey are very differentMeaningless. Inter-cluster distance is not preserved.
One cluster is biggerIt is more spread out / more diverseMeaningless. Cluster size reflects local density, not extent.
A cluster appearedThere is real group structureSometimes an artifact — t-SNE can produce clusters from pure noise at low perplexity.
B sits next to AB is one of A's nearest neighboursThis one is usually trustworthy. It is the only thing the algorithm optimises for.

And two more properties worth internalising: t-SNE is stochastic, so two runs on identical data give different pictures; and it is non-parametric, meaning there is no learned function to apply to a new point. Adding one node means re-running, and everything moves.

The disqualifying property for a map

A map whose geography reshuffles every time a document is ingested is not a map. It is a fresh picture each time that happens to use the same data. If a user has learned where things are, that learning is destroyed on every update — and spatial memory is most of what makes a map more useful than a list.

06

UMAP and what came after

UMAP (McInnes, Healy & Melville, 2018) reaches similar pictures from a different starting point. Instead of probabilities, it builds a weighted k-nearest-neighbour graph and treats it as a fuzzy topological structure, then optimises a low-dimensional graph to match using cross-entropy with negative sampling. In practice, three differences matter:

  • Speed. Approximate nearest-neighbour search plus negative sampling makes it scale to millions of points where t-SNE strains.
  • More global structure survives. Relative positions of clusters carry somewhat more meaning than in t-SNE. Somewhat — this is a matter of degree, and it is still not safe to read inter-cluster distance as similarity.
  • It can be made a function. UMAP supports transforming new points using an existing embedding, and parametric variants learn an explicit neural mapping. This is the property that would let a method like this coexist with a stable map.

Newer work — PaCMAP and TriMap among others — is explicitly motivated by the local/global tradeoff, attempting to preserve more of the coarse structure without giving up neighbourhoods. The honest summary of the field is that no projection preserves everything, and every method is a statement about which distortion you find acceptable.

So: do people still use t-SNE? Yes, widely, particularly where local cluster structure is the whole point. But it is no longer the default reach for exploratory work, and it is a poor fit anywhere the picture must be stable across runs or updates.

07

Measuring what survived

Everything above is qualitative until you measure it, so let us measure it. The natural quantity is neighbourhood preservation: for each node, take its k nearest neighbours in the original 768-dimensional space, take its k nearest in the 2-d picture, and ask what fraction overlap. Average over all nodes. A score of 1.0 means every local neighbourhood survived the flattening intact; 0.0 means none did.

Random guessing gives roughly k/(n−1), which for these settings is about 4% — that is the floor any method has to beat.

The force layout's score is the interesting one. It is built from graph edges and has no access to the embeddings at all, so whatever it scores measures how much your graph's link structure agrees with your embedding space. If the two agreed perfectly you would not need both.

The result, at k = 10

Chance is 3.9%. An arbitrary pair of dimensions gets 8.7%. PCA gets 15.2%. t-SNE gets 42.1%. The force layout gets 6.8% — barely above chance, and worse than picking two dimensions at random.

That is not a criticism of force layout, which is optimising something else entirely and doing it well. It is a measurement of how little the two structures overlap: the graph's edges and the embedding's geometry are close to independent sources of information about the same nodes. Which means a layout using only one of them is leaving the other entirely on the floor.

08

What this implies for a map you have to keep

Pulling the threads together for the specific case of a semantic-zoom knowledge graph that must stay stable across ingests:

  1. A global non-linear projection is the wrong primary layout, not because it produces bad pictures but because re-fitting moves everything. The requirement that a learned map stays put is a harder constraint than picture quality, and it eliminates most of the field outright.
  2. The embeddings are still the right raw material. The failure mode of a pure force layout is that a node with no edges feels no forces, so it sits wherever it was put. Every node has a vector, including the ones with no edges. That asymmetry is the opportunity.
  3. The synthesis is springs, not projection. Build a k-nearest-neighbour graph from the embeddings and feed those as additional springs into the force layout you already run. You keep incremental settling — a new node attaches to its neighbours and relaxes locally, without disturbing the rest — and you get embedding structure into the geometry. Notice this is what t-SNE's optimiser is already doing under the hood; the difference is that you keep the ability to add one point at a time.
  4. If you want a projection anyway, freeze it. Fit PCA (or parametric UMAP) once on a reference snapshot, store the mapping, and apply it to new nodes as a pure function. Stability comes from refusing to re-fit, which is a policy decision, not an algorithmic one.

The one-line version

Projection methods answer "where should everything go, given everything?" A map that has to stay put needs the answer to a different question: "where should this new thing go, given where everything already is?" Those are not the same question, and only the second one composes.

09

What the perception literature knows, and mostly doesn't

Everything above is about what a projection preserves mathematically. None of it says anything about what a person can read off the result. That is a separate literature, and its most important property is how thin it is.

Ellis & Dix surveyed clutter-reduction techniques and found that 12 of 65 papers reported any evaluation at all. Almost every decluttering method in common use is an untested proposal that spread because it was plausible. Hold that thought while reading the rest of this section — including the parts that support what this project already built.

Opacity has a much narrower usable band than intuition suggests

This is the finding that changed code in this repository. Harrison, Ishii, Vicente & Buxton (CHI '95, pp. 317–324) measured how much a faded item interferes with the item you are actually attending to, using a Stroop paradigm. Aboelsaadat & Balakrishnan (British HCI 2004, pp. 53–67, N=16) independently reproduced the breakpoints nine years later. Both found the same two things:

StudyInterference groupings (post-hoc, α=.05)
Harrison et al. 1995{100, 50, 20} | 10 | 5 | 0
Aboelsaadat & Balakrishnan 2004{100, 75, 50, 25} | {10, 7.5} | {5}
  1. The curve is flat from roughly 25% to 100%. Fading something from 1.0 to 0.5 buys almost nothing perceptually. Every intermediate alpha above 0.25 is spent for no return.
  2. Alpha near 0.5 sits close to the peak of the interference curve — the most distracting place to put a de-emphasized item, not a polite middle.

Bartram & Stone (IEEE TVCG 17(10):1444–1458, 2011) asked the boundary-condition question directly and give ~0.1 for backgrounds that are not too dense, 0.2 universally. They name the top-of-range failure the fence effect: past a threshold, backgrounded structure stops reading as a layer behind the data and starts reading as a barrier in front of it.

This corrected shipped code

The node-fade added to this project's viewer originally used tier floors of 0.30 and 0.60. Both sit in the dead zone; the 0.60 tier looked like a fade and did essentially nothing, and mid-degree nodes at one zoom level landed near 0.48, which is close to the worst available value. The mapping now uses two regimes — backgrounded inside 0.10–0.20 where ordering is still legible, then a fast crossing to present — because the evidence says there are only two useful states.

Stated as extrapolation, because it is one. Every measurement above used thin reference structures such as grid lines, or Stroop stimuli. Nobody has run this on de-emphasized content items in a working display. And one boundary is known: Mairena, Gutwin & Cockburn (Information Visualization 21(2):95–129, 2022) tested 12 emphasis effects across 6 magnitudes and found opacity de-emphasis fails outright on space-filling layouts — maximum accuracy 0.56 on a HexbinMap at any magnitude. Discrete marks are fine; filled regions are not.

Edge bundling distorts the thing it is used to reveal

The folk wisdom is that bundling reveals high-level structure. The evidence says otherwise, and the strongest study is recent. Wallinger, Akbulut, Rufai, Purchase & Archambault (CHI '25) found participants preferred bundled views, followed false connections in tightly bundled diagrams, and — the finding that matters — bundle routing changed which nodes participants assigned to clusters. Bundling distorts the cluster reading you would adopt it to produce.

Bach et al. (IEEE TVCG 23(1):541–550, 2017, N=15) measured shortest-path performance across four edge representations and found bundling significantly the worst: 17.2 s versus 9.3 s for plain graphs, accuracy 77% versus 86% — on graphs of 7 to 15 nodes. It hurts at trivial scale. Holten's original 2006 paper self-describes its evaluation as informal; Force-Directed Edge Bundling (2009) has no user study at all.

Structural metrics do not predict what people see

Wu, Cao, Archambault, Shen, Qu & Cui (IEEE TVCG 23(1):401–410, 2017) ran three controlled experiments on graph sampling and found that of many computed cluster-quality metrics, only cluster count matched perceived quality. Participants also felt one sampling method covered more of the display than another when both covered similar pixel percentages.

The warning transfers directly to anything in this tutorial: do not tune a decluttering heuristic against a structural graph metric and assume the perceptual result follows. It does not. And relatedly, Holten, Isenberg, van Wijk & Fekete (PacificVis 2011, pp. 195–202) found that stacking visual cues "did not result in significant performance gains, hinting at possible interference instead of reinforcement" — combining fade with desaturation with shrink may not compound.

Semantic zoom itself has never been evaluated

Worth ending on, because it reframes everything above. Semantic zoom has been a named, central concept since Pad (Perlin & Fox, 1993), and is treated as a defining dimension of zoomable interfaces in the field's standard survey (Cockburn, Karlson & Bederson, ACM Computing Surveys 41(1), 2008). Yet that survey's empirical-evaluations section discusses semantic zoom in exactly one study, as a secondary factor, and its summary does not mention it at all. No controlled study isolates semantic zoom against geometric zoom, and none exists for zoomable graph interfaces at all.

The one substantive result to lean on: Hornbæk, Bederson & Plaisant (ACM TOCHI 9(4):362–389, 2002, N=32) found that with semantic zoom enabled, adding an overview made users slower, apparently because the semantic detail made the overview redundant. Notably, 80% preferred the overview anyway — a clean demonstration that preference and performance dissociate. Treat it as suggestive: the primary text is paywalled and secondary sources disagree about whether semantic zoom was a controlled factor or a property of one of the two test maps.

The honest summary

You are designing in a field where the central concepts are thirty years old, universally cited, and largely unevaluated. That is not a reason for paralysis. It is a reason to measure your own system, since there is often nothing to look up — and it means a careful measurement on a real corpus can be worth more than a citation.
