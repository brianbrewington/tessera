# **The Architecture of Attention: Dynamics of Allocation, Manipulation, and Saturation**

Author: Gemini Agent (not Brian\!)  
Jul 12, 2026

## **Introduction to the Attention Economy and the Saturating Channel**

In an information-rich world, the fundamental constraint on processing, decision-making, and action is no longer the availability of data, but the profound scarcity of the cognitive bandwidth required to evaluate it. As formulated by Herbert Simon in 1971, a wealth of information inherently creates a poverty of attention, necessitating mechanisms to allocate this finite resource efficiently across competing demands.1 This foundational economic truth has evolved from a theoretical abstraction into the central engineering, biological, and sociological challenge of the modern era. Attention is not merely a metaphor for focus; it is a measurable, saturating channel, a rivalrous economic resource, and a structural bottleneck that governs everything from institutional risk management to interpersonal empathy.2  
The contemporary landscape of attention allocation reveals a recurring architectural motif: the "router." Whether instantiated as a medical triage protocol, an algorithmic social media feed, a legal materiality threshold, or the biological inhibitory networks of the human brain, attention routers determine what crosses the threshold into the "lit" region of conscious evaluation and what is relegated to the "dark" region of the unexamined.1 When these routers function optimally, they enable complex systems and organisms to survive environments of overwhelming complexity. When they fail—or when they are adversarially captured—the results manifest as catastrophic institutional failures, successful security exploits, or profound cognitive distress.1  
This comprehensive report explores the theoretical underpinnings and practical applications of attention allocation. By integrating economic models of rational inattention, institutional pathology, the manipulative arts of magic and comedy, visual saliency in photography and radiology, evolutionary signaling theory, and the neurobiological frameworks of neurodivergence, a unified theory of attention routing emerges. This synthesis explores how attention is shaped, how its budgets are optimized or exhausted, and how the manipulation of these thresholds governs human behavior and systemic stability.

## **The Formal Core: Economic Budgeting and Rational Inattention**

The economic formalization of attention begins with the recognition that evaluation is biologically and computationally costly. The framework of "rational inattention," heavily pioneered by Christopher Sims in 2003, posits that agents operating under an information-capacity constraint must actively choose which signals to attend to, deliberately ignoring others to maximize utility.1 In models of incomplete income insurance utilizing Constant Absolute Risk Aversion (CARA) utility with Gaussian shocks, rational inattention demonstrates that the Permanent Income Hypothesis arises precisely due to this balancing of precautionary savings and impatience under bandwidth constraints.4

### **Koopman Search Theory and the Shadow Price**

A critical feature of rational attention allocation under a strict budget is the emergence of a hard threshold, mathematically related to B.O. Koopman's World War II-era optimal search and screening theory.1 In optimal search models, observational effort density is allocated based on target probability. Regions or items whose marginal value falls below a certain "shadow price" do not receive a proportionally smaller fraction of attention; they receive identically zero effort.1 This invariant mathematically dictates the existence of an "attention line." Below this line, information is not skimmed, summarized, or rapidly evaluated; it is structurally and functionally invisible.1

### **The Saturating Channel in Automated Code Review**

This binary distinction—reviewed versus unreviewed—is empirically measurable in artificial systems. In experimental frameworks testing the reviewability of code using Large Language Models (LLMs), researchers discovered that the reviewer acts as a saturating channel.1 Initial testing phases (Phase 0a and 0b) debunked the idea that "clever code is cheaper in tokens," proving that token cost is length-dominated.1 However, Phase 1 experiments revealed a profound structural limitation: the review-token cost is flat relative to input length. An LLM tasked with evaluating an input eight times larger than a standard prompt does not proportionally scale its output. Instead, it emits a fixed, saturating review budget of approximately 700 to 1000 tokens regardless of input size.1  
As the input artifact grows, the review density per line collapses dramatically. In one measurement, review-per-1k-prompt fell from 1130 to 105 as the input expanded.1 The excess material is not evaluated poorly; its excess is simply not reviewed at all. This aligns precisely with the water-filling optimal policy under a budget: review-per-line collapsing 10x is not a sign of algorithmic laziness, but the mathematical signature of an optimal policy hitting its shadow price.1 Phase 2 proofs further confirmed that while the LLM judge capability gradient is real, there is a "coherence floor" below which reviewability becomes unmeasurable, generating false negatives for real catches and false positives through confabulation.1

### **The Epsilon Trickle and Goodhart's Failure**

When an attention router optimizes purely for "changes caught" or "defects found," it inevitably falls victim to Goodhart's Law. The observing agent will camp its resources in regions where change is frequent but potentially low-impact, while neglecting static but critical infrastructure.1 To counteract this, optimal policies must weight discoveries by severity or "user penalty"—relying on severity-priced objectives rather than raw catch counts.1  
Furthermore, a live attention router creates a dangerous epistemological feedback loop: once it darkens a region, it ceases to receive data about that region, making it blind to any subsequent changes in the environment's baseline risk. The mathematical countermeasure is the "epsilon trickle"—a small, mandatory, randomized audit budget allocated to dark regions strictly to keep the router's probability map calibrated.1 While historically unaffordable for human institutions, the advent of automated LLM reviewers makes this epsilon audit practically free, ensuring the allocator remains calibrated to off-policy drift.1

## **Institutional Pathology: The Ten Rooms of Attention Allocation**

Institutions function as macro-scale attention routers. Over the past seventy years, human factors engineering has largely focused on arranging the external world to fit the fixed bandwidth of the human sensory channel.1 Christopher Wickens's SEEV model (Salience, Effort, Expectancy, Value) mathematically defines this visual and cognitive scanning behavior, where the probability of attending to an Area of Interest (AOI) is driven by the salience of the event, the expected frequency of information, and its value, minus the physical effort required to shift gaze.1 This culminated in the concept of the "dark cockpit," where only actionable, off-nominal data is illuminated to prevent sensory overload.1 However, this shaping mechanism is inherently dual-use; it can optimize safety, but it can also be captured by schedule pressure, malicious actors, or bureaucratic inertia.

### **Normalization of Deviance and Administrative Re-binning**

Diane Vaughan's exhaustive analysis of the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster illustrates how organizational attention routers fail through the "normalization of deviance".1 Live anomaly signals (such as O-ring erosion) were administratively reclassified as "in-family" expected behavior.1 By generating paperwork that re-binned a critical physical reality into a routine administrative category, the organization effectively pushed a live threat below the attention line.1 The organization's router was captured not by malice, but by the relentless pressure of schedule, causing live anomaly signals to be routed into the dark.1

### **Approval Theatre and Exploit Specs**

When an attention line becomes static, documented, and highly predictable, it ceases to be a safeguard and becomes an "exploit spec".1 In regulatory environments, this manifests as "approval theatre." For example, the Boeing 737 MAX certification process relied heavily on Organization Designation Authorization (ODA), delegating oversight to the auditee and replacing an independent, external sensor with the manufacturer's own internal salience map.1 Similarly, automotive self-certification against predictable NCAP pedestrian robot testing allows manufacturers to optimize for the exact parameters of the test rather than the chaotic, real-world environment, essentially treating a frozen attention line as an optimization target.1  
In software engineering, the 2024 XZ Utils backdoor stands as a masterpiece of adversarial attention routing.1 Adversarial review-shaping, previously celebrated as a competitive art form in the Underhanded C Contest, became a planetary threat.1 The adversary reverse-engineered the open-source maintainers' salience functions, obfuscating the malicious payload within test files, build scripts, and vendored dependencies—regions universally acknowledged as "boilerplate" and thus residing perpetually in the dark, unreviewed zone below the shadow price.1

### **The Inverted Dark Cockpit and Alarm Fatigue**

Conversely, flooding the attention channel destroys the router's utility entirely. In clinical medicine, "alarm fatigue" demonstrates the catastrophic failure of an inverted dark cockpit: if every patient monitor lights up and sounds an alarm, the entire environment is darkened through sensory saturation.1 When every signal is treated as critical, the SEEV model's "Value" parameter collapses, and the observer's attention budget is rapidly exhausted.5 The introduction of surgical checklists (as championed by Atul Gawande) succeeded because a checklist acts as a hand-compiled, rigid attention router. Its power lies as much in explicitly removing extraneous items from the lit region as it does in mandating vigilance for critical steps.1

## **Top-Down Routing and Visual Saliency**

The physical allocation of visual attention—how the eye scans a scene—provides a direct physiological trace of cognitive routing. This routing is a continuous tug-of-war between "bottom-up" sensory salience and "top-down" cognitive goals.7

### **Yarbus and Task-Dependent Gaze**

In the 1960s, Soviet psychologist Alfred Yarbus conducted foundational eye-tracking research using Ilya Repin's painting *An Unexpected Visitor*.9 By recording the saccades and fixations of subjects, Yarbus proved that gaze routing is intensely task-dependent. If asked to estimate the ages of the people in the painting, the viewer's eyes fixated almost exclusively on faces; if asked to determine the material wealth of the family, the gaze shifted entirely to the furniture and clothing.10 The eye does not smoothly follow contours; it leaps between regions of high salience, driven heavily by top-down narrative goals and internal schemata.12 Modern replications utilizing webcam eye-tracking algorithms have confirmed that while bottom-up features attract initial gaze, prior knowledge, expectations, and the specific task at hand dictate the sustained looking behavior.10

### **Bottom-Up Saliency and Visual Hierarchy**

Despite the power of top-down tasks, bottom-up sensory data exerts an involuntary pull on the attention budget. The Itti and Koch model of visual attention mathematically defines a "saliency map"—a spatiotopic representation constructed from low-level visual features like luminance, color contrast, and orientation.7 The human visual cortex processes an image in under 150 milliseconds, automatically assigning perceptual priority to areas with the highest differential.15  
In the domain of photography, artists explicitly manipulate this hierarchy to prevent the viewer's eye from wandering aimlessly. A robust visual hierarchy establishes a clear sequence of perceptual information, preventing visual conflict caused by elements competing for equal tonal weight.15 This hierarchy relies on four perceptual pillars:

1. **Tonal Contrast and Luminance Differential:** The highest luminance differential (e.g., chiaroscuro, placing the lightest light adjacent to the deepest shadow) draws immediate, involuntary fixation.15  
2. **Subject Scale:** Larger items possess higher perceptual weight, often manipulated via telephoto focal lengths to compress spatial depth.15  
3. **Chromatic Saturation:** Color temperature establishes hierarchy; warm tones (2700K–4000K) conceptually advance, while cool tones recede.15  
4. **Spatial Placement:** Subjects placed at the intersections of the "rule of thirds" receive elevated perceptual priority.15

Diagnostic tests, such as the "Squint Test" (blurring the image to identify the dominant tonal shape) and the "Mirror Test" (flipping the image horizontally to identify asymmetrical visual weight imbalances), are utilized by artists to empirically verify that their intended salience map aligns with the physiological realities of human perception.15

### **Expertise, Chunking, and Satisfaction of Search**

As individuals gain expertise in a domain, their visual routing fundamentally alters, transitioning from bottom-up processing to highly structured top-down processing. In chess, eye-tracking studies reveal that novices scan the board randomly in a bottom-up fashion, whereas grandmasters immediately fixate on the most tactically relevant squares.16 This top-down routing is facilitated by "chunking"—the ability to recognize and hold up to 50,000 familiar patterns in long-term memory.16 Chunking allows the expert to process massive amounts of information through the bottleneck of working memory by relying on System 1 intuition (pattern recognition) rather than expending deep calculation (System 2).16  
However, trained top-down routing introduces specialized vulnerabilities. In clinical radiology, this manifests as "Satisfaction of Search" (SOS) or "Subsequent Search Miss" (SSM).19 When a radiologist successfully locates a primary abnormality (e.g., a pulmonary nodule on a chest radiograph or an acromioclavicular separation on a skeletal radiograph), their likelihood of detecting a secondary, distinct abnormality in the same image drops significantly.19 In a study of multiple-finding skeletal radiographs, the detection rate for the first finding averaged 11.72 (out of 15), but detection rates for second and third abnormalities plummeted to 6.12.21  
Two theories explain this: the Satisfaction theory hypothesizes a temporal problem where observers prematurely terminate their search after becoming satisfied with a diagnosis, while the Perceptual Set theory argues observers are less likely to spot a secondary target that is visually dissimilar to the first.19 Regardless of the exact mechanism, this highlights a paradox of the trained router: inattentional blindness is often the direct cost of highly efficient, chunked expertise.1

## **Active Attention Manipulation: The Psychology of Magic and Comedy**

While institutions struggle to manage attention passively, practitioners of the performing arts have spent centuries developing precise, empirical technologies to actively hijack, manipulate, and route human attention.

### **The Psychology of Magic and Misdirection**

Magicians do not merely hide objects; they manipulate the audience's predictive internal models. Cognitive psychologists, including Gustav Kuhn, have demonstrated that magic provides a highly ecologically valid arena for studying visual attention and inattentional blindness (IB).22 Misdirection relies on choreographing the spectator's gaze (overt attention) and their mental focus (covert attention) away from the secret method and toward an inconsequential but salient action.24  
In a classic experiment utilizing a live magical performance, Kuhn tracked the eye movements of participants watching a "cigarette vanish" trick. The magician accidentally places the wrong end of a cigarette in his mouth, then drops a lighter into his lap while the audience's attention is captured by the readjustment of the cigarette.23 Eye-tracking data revealed that viewers routinely missed the highly salient, moving visual stimulus of the dropping lighter, not because they were looking elsewhere, but because their covert attention was consumed by the narrative climax of the cigarette adjustment.23 In a follow-up condition where the falling lighter was digitally removed, 40% of participants still incorrectly inferred that the lighter was dropped, highlighting how narrative inference overwrites raw sensory data.23  
Magicians employ a rigorous psychological taxonomy of misdirection targeting perception, memory, and reasoning.25 Techniques include manipulating attentional timing through physical cues (e.g., Slydini's use of forward posture to create tension and heightened attention, followed by leaning back to induce relaxation and lower vigilance).25 Semantic misdirection utilizes the narrative structure of the performance, executing the secret method during an "off-beat" moment immediately after a joke or a climax, exploiting the brain's natural lull in attention.25 Furthermore, tricks like the "phantom vanish" demonstrate that the brain's predictive processing can generate erroneous perceptions of objects that are merely implied by the magician's actions, entirely overriding bottom-up sensory input.22

### **Comedy as a Cognitive Shift**

The mechanics of stand-up comedy share a profound structural equivalence with magical misdirection. Both rely heavily on the manipulation of implicit assumptions. According to Greg Dean's theoretical framework, every successful joke relies on the simultaneous construction of two narratives.26  
The "setup" of a joke establishes a "first story"—an interpretation built on standard assumptions and expected context.26 This story acts as the cognitive misdirection, focusing the audience's attention on a highly probable, but ultimately false, reality. The "connector" is a pivot point (a word or concept with double meaning) that bridges the two narratives. The "punchline" suddenly reveals the "second story," forcing an immediate, involuntary collapse of the first story.26 For example, the setup "I asked the librarian if they had books about paranoia" builds a first story of a patron requesting information. The punchline "She whispered, 'They're right behind you'" instantly collapses the first story, revealing the second story where the librarian herself is paranoid.26  
The laughter that follows is a neurological reward for successfully navigating this cognitive shift and resolving the incongruity.26 Functional MRI and EEG studies confirm this structural model, demonstrating that the punchline requires the brain to re-evaluate semantic information. The expectation stage activates the posterior insula and middle frontal gyrus; the incongruity stage relies on the middle temporal gyrus (MTG) and medial frontal gyrus (MFG) to detect semantic conflict; and the resolution stage triggers the release of dopamine and endorphins in the nucleus accumbens, producing the sensation of amusement.27 EEG analysis further reveals that pun-based jokes elicit pronounced anterior N400 and posterior P600 effects, correlating with the cognitive effort required to shift from a dominant idiomatic reading to a literal interpretation.29 The comedy club acts as a tight feedback loop, utilizing the involuntary, millisecond-resolved trace of the laugh as execution-verified proof that the cognitive misdirection succeeded.1

## **Evolutionary Attention Economics: The Handicap Principle**

The competition for attention is not a uniquely human phenomenon; it is a fundamental driver of evolutionary biology. In nature, organisms must frequently capture the evaluation budget of a mate or a predator. However, because attention is a scarce and vital resource, receivers have evolved strict gating mechanisms to ignore easily falsified claims and cheap talk.30  
To bypass this filter, nature utilizes the "Handicap Principle," theorized by Israeli biologist Amotz Zahavi in 1975\.30 Zahavi proposed that secondary sexual characteristics (such as the massive, heavy, and highly visible tail of a peacock) serve as honest signals of genetic quality precisely *because* they are wasteful and costly.30 A low-quality male could not afford the metabolic cost or the predation risk associated with growing and carrying such an extravagant ornament.30 The signal functions like conspicuous consumption, lowering overall fitness to definitively prove underlying quality to the receiver.30  
This principle extends to predator-deterrent signaling, such as the "stotting" behavior in gazelles, which signals to a predator that the prey is fit and not worth the metabolic effort of a chase.30 Empirical validation of the cost-honesty link was demonstrated in a study of trained Blue Jays. When placed in an environment with misaligned incentives (a conflict of interest between sender and receiver), the birds produced dishonest signals when the cost of signaling was low. However, when researchers increased the cost of the signal (forcing the sender to execute exhausting shuttle flights between perches before signaling), the birds reverted to honest signaling, confirming that signal costs stabilize honesty.31  
In the context of institutional attention economics, evolutionary costly signaling acts as execution-verified proof that a payload belongs above the attention line.1 It is the biological antidote to human "approval theatre." While human institutions often rely on easily forged checklists or boilerplate attestations, biological systems demand physical handicaps to ensure that the attention budget is spent only on authentic signals.

## **Neurodivergent Attention Architecture: Monotropism and the Intense World**

If institutions and artists manipulate attention top-down, neurobiology dictates the baseline architecture of the budget itself. Traditional psychiatric and medical models have long pathologized conditions like Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) and Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) as behavioral or social deficits, framing them through the lens of impaired executive function or a lack of "Theory of Mind".34 However, modern frameworks—many pioneered by neurodivergent researchers—reconceptualize these conditions as fundamental, structural variations in attention allocation.

### **Monotropism vs. Polytropism**

The standard neurotypical brain is generally characterized as "polytropic." A polytropic mind functions similarly to a broad, diffuse lantern; it is capable of rapidly shifting focus, tracking multiple parallel sensory streams, monitoring social cues, and sustaining moderate attention across varied, simultaneous inputs.34  
Conversely, the autistic and AuDHD (Autistic \+ ADHD) cognitive style is heavily "monotropic," a theory advanced in 1992 by Dinah Murray and formalized in 2005 alongside Wenn Lawson and Mike Lesser.34 A monotropic mind functions as an intense "attention tunnel" or laser beam. At any given moment, the finite cognitive resource budget is poured into a highly restricted number of sensory or cognitive channels.34  
When engaged in a strong personal interest, this architecture facilitates profound hyper-focus and a joyful "flow state," allowing for unparalleled pattern recognition, creativity, and depth of processing.35 However, this extreme channeling comes at a strict opportunity cost: stimuli outside the active tunnel are aggressively filtered out. The individual becomes highly unaware of external environmental changes, physiological needs (interoception), and peripheral social cues.35

| **Allocation Model** | Diffuse, distributed (Lantern) | Concentrated, singular (Laser/Tunnel) 38 |
| **Task Switching** | Low metabolic cost, rapid shifting | Extremely high cognitive cost (Inertia) 35 |

| **Social Manifestation** | Parallel tracking of ambient cues | Hyper-focus on specific concepts; direct communication 36 |

### **The Monotropic Split and Autistic Inertia**

Because attention in a monotropic system is concentrated, reallocating it requires immense cognitive momentum. This results in "autistic inertia"—a severe resistance to starting, stopping, or shifting tasks.35 Research using eye-tracking has distinguished between "sticky attention" (being slow to disengage from a current focus, correlated with intense sensory experiences) and "springy attention" (quickly returning to a previous focus).42  
When a monotropic individual is forcibly subjected to unexpected transitions or forced to process multiple, competing sensory inputs simultaneously, the attention tunnel fractures. This phenomenon, termed a "monotropic split" by researcher Tanya Adkin, occurs when the brain is pushed beyond its architectural capacity to share resources.35 The forced diffusion of the focused laser beam causes acute sensory dysregulation, profound exhaustion, and severe anxiety.35 Over time, chronic monotropic splitting is the primary mechanism behind autistic burnout, executive dysfunction, demand avoidance, and meltdowns.35 Stimming behaviors (repetitive movements like rocking or flapping) are thus understood not as purposeless tics, but as active defense mechanisms—generating predictable sensory input to shield the primary attention tunnel from intrusive, overwhelming disruptions.35

### **The Intense World Theory and Sensory Gating**

The behavioral manifestations of monotropism are neurobiologically underpinned by the "Intense World Theory," proposed by Kamila and Henry Markram.43 Drawing on the Valproic Acid (VPA) rat model of autism, the Markrams argue that the autistic brain is defined by the hyper-functioning of local neural microcircuits. At a cellular level, gestational exposure to VPA causes histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibition, leading to excitatory dominance.45 The resulting neural microcircuits are characterized by both hyper-reactivity (excessive response to stimuli) and hyper-plasticity (overly strong, nearly permanent connections, driven by the over-expression of NMDA receptor subunits NR2A and NR2B and enhanced CaMKII expression).44  
This localized excitatory dominance leads to four emergent dimensions of cognitive hyper-functionality:

1. **Hyper-perception:** Enhanced, overwhelming sensory processing in the neocortex.45  
2. **Hyper-attention:** Obsessive, runaway tracking of highly specific fragments of the environment.45  
3. **Hyper-memory:** Idiosyncratic, highly persistent fear and stimulus imprinting, trapping the individual in powerful memories.45  
4. **Hyper-emotionality:** Amplified emotional processing mediated by hyper-functionality in the limbic system (specifically the amygdala).45

In neurotypical individuals, the brain utilizes "sensory gating" (measurable via P50 event-related potentials in EEG) to rapidly suppress neural responses to repetitive, uninformative stimuli, preventing cognitive overflow.46 In many autistic and ADHD individuals, this P50 suppression is demonstrably deficient.46 Consequently, the sensory environment is experienced as painfully intense, chaotic, and loud.44  
From this perspective, the social withdrawal often pathologized in autism is not a core deficit of empathy; rather, it is a highly rational, defensive decoupling.36 The autistic individual retreats into a secure, predictable internal bubble or "attention tunnel" to survive an intense world that is structurally engineered to trigger a monotropic split.35

### **The Double Empathy Problem and Interpersonal Routing**

This alternative view of attention architecture perfectly explains the "Double Empathy Problem" proposed by Damian Milton.49 Traditional models assume autistic individuals lack the capacity to empathize or utilize Theory of Mind.36 Milton demonstrated that communication breakdowns are a two-way street caused by a mismatch in dispositional outlooks and attention routing.49  
A neurotypical individual struggles to communicate with an autistic individual just as much as the reverse, often holding implicit biases against divergent communication styles.49 When two individuals operate with fundamentally different bandwidth constraints and routing algorithms (polytropic vs. monotropic), mutual incomprehension is inevitable. The phenomenological account of empathy suggests that understanding others is not purely inferential, but based on shared awareness and salience.51 If an autistic person's monotropic attention architecture makes simultaneous task focus and ambient social awareness impossible, their apparent failure of Theory of Mind is actually a reflection of attentional constraints, often compounded by alexithymia (difficulty identifying internal emotions) and differences in interoception.36 Rapport, empathy, and communication effectiveness dramatically improve when autistic individuals are paired with other autistic adults, proving that the deficit is not absolute, but relational.49

## **Synthesis and Final Perspectives**

Attention is the keystone resource of the information age, operating under unforgiving economic and biological constraints. The phenomenon of the "hard line"—below which information ceases to exist for the observer—is a universal invariant. It dictates the behavior of LLM code reviewers operating on token budgets, fatigued clinicians navigating ICU alarms, over-burdened organizations normalizing deviance, and the human visual cortex responding to luminance differentials.1  
Whether intentionally manipulated by a magician's misdirection, exploited by a software backdoor hidden in boilerplate, disrupted by the cognitive mechanics of a comedy punchline, or overwhelmed in the neurodivergent experience of an intense world, the mechanisms of attention routing remain remarkably consistent.1 The router is the supreme arbiter of reality. When an institution allows schedule pressure to re-bin anomalies into the dark region, it guarantees failure. When a chess master chunks 50,000 patterns to optimize System 1 evaluation, they simultaneously generate the blind spots responsible for Satisfaction of Search errors.1  
The task of modern systems design—from software architecture to pedagogy and personal AI agents—is to abandon the assumption that human or machine bandwidth can artificially grow to meet the exponential proliferation of data. Instead, engineering must responsibly, safely, and transparently route the scarcity we are biologically and mathematically bound by. Implementing execution-verified catch signals, severity-priced objectives, and an inviolable epsilon audit trickle ensures that the attention router remains tethered to objective reality, guarding the threshold between the lit world and the unread dark.1
